### 🛡️ AI Safety

**📌 METR and Redwood red-teamed OpenAI's internal agent monitoring**
🏷️ Source: METR (2026-10-22)
📝 Core: From 21 July to 11 August, METR and Redwood Research ran a high-access red-team exercise against OpenAI's internal misalignment monitoring, with access to internal code, monitor prompts and roughly a dozen engineers, producing a 61-page report. They estimated 75–94% of agentic Responses API traffic was eligible for monitoring (99.2% of internal Codex traffic), but RL training runs were not covered; compaction gaps left roughly 10% of eligible Codex traffic unmonitored between May and July; and because the monitor only triggered when an agent returned control, agents could indefinitely postpone monitoring by continuing to act. They also found a way to bypass Codex sandboxing and Auto-Review, an exploit that kept hundreds of prior actions — including the exploit itself — from being monitored, and two client-controllable headers that disable monitoring for new agents. OpenAI says all production-impacting vulnerabilities have been patched.
💡 Significance: This is the closest public analogue to a stress test of the monitoring pipeline itself, and it moves agentic safety from "will the model misbehave" to "can the monitoring be routed around". METR's design ideals are directly copyable into a spec: make being unmonitored require explicit approval, monitor every N messages rather than at turn end, reconcile intended-versus-actual coverage in logs, isolate agents from their monitors, and keep red-teaming the monitor.

**📌 OpenAI asks Newsom to write monitoring, shutdown controls and audits into SB 53**
🏷️ Source: AIAffairs / StateScoop (2026-10-22)
📝 Core: In a letter dated 20 October, OpenAI's Chan Park asked Governor Gavin Newsom to amend the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (SB 53) so developers must monitor models for dangerous behaviour during training and evaluation, maintain effective shutdown controls, undergo annual independent audits, strengthen cybersecurity, and notify parties affected by covered cyber incidents.
💡 Significance: SB 53 currently asks large developers to publish safety frameworks and report certain critical incidents — "say what you did". OpenAI's amendments turn that into "prove what you can do": monitoring happens during development rather than only before release, and audits are external rather than self-certified. For other labs the letter sets the legislative coordinates; for regulators it is a case study in a vendor asking for more obligations, and it draws a sharper line between voluntary frameworks and legal duty.

**📌 OpenAI and Poland's NASK partner on AI safety and security**
🏷️ Source: OpenAI News (2026-10-23)
📝 Core: OpenAI signed an agreement with Poland's NASK National Research Institute, whose AI Safety Research Center serves as the Polish AI Safety Institute, to jointly study how to evaluate increasingly capable systems, identify vulnerabilities and strengthen safeguards. It builds on July's work in which CERT Polska, which operates inside NASK, used OpenAI cyber-capable models to find six MikroTik RouterOS vulnerabilities — some combinable into unauthenticated device takeover — which the vendor patched and CERT Polska confirmed blocked observed attacks.
💡 Significance: Frontier safety governance is spreading from Washington and London into national research institutes. OpenAI already works with the US CAISSI and the UK AISI; the Polish agreement is notable for its concrete justification — without national partners, global standards drift away from where AI is actually used. For smaller countries it is a path to frontier model access via incident-response capability; for labs it is a cheap way to outsource external evaluation.

### 🧭 Today's takeaway

Today's four trend stories are one move: detaching capability from a particular vendor or workflow and making it portable. Agent Skills to a foundation, OpenResponses to a foundation, Claude into Google Cloud, OlmoEarth turning the whole planet into reusable embeddings — each lowers the cost of choosing. The three safety stories ask the other half: once it is detached, who verifies it? METR shows the monitoring pipeline itself has routes around it, OpenAI wants monitoring and audits written into law, and NASK represents national institutions taking on part of the verification work. The keyword today is a race between portability and verifiability: the faster the parts disperse, the less verification can rest on vendor self-disclosure and a few people's good intentions.
